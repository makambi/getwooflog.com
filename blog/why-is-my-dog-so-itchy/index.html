Why Is My Dog So Itchy? Causes, When to Worry, What to Do

General information for dog owners · Last updated June 4, 2026

Most itching in dogs comes down to one of three things: fleas and other parasites, allergies (to things in the environment or in food), or a skin infection. A bit of occasional scratching is normal, but constant itching — or skin that looks red, broken, or smells off — is your dog telling you something needs a closer look.

This article explains the common causes in plain English, the red flags that mean call your vet now, and what to note down so your appointment is as useful as possible. It is general information, not a diagnosis or a substitute for veterinary care.

Likely causes, in plain English

Itching (vets call it pruritus) is a symptom, not a diagnosis. Several different problems can look identical from the outside, which is why guessing rarely helps. The usual suspects:

  • Fleas and flea-allergy dermatitis. The single most common cause. Some dogs are so sensitive that a single bite triggers days of intense itching, often around the rump, tail base, and back legs — and you may never see a live flea.
  • Environmental allergies (atopic dermatitis). Reactions to pollen, dust mites, mold, or grasses. These often flare seasonally and tend to affect the paws, face, ears, armpits, and belly.
  • Food allergy or sensitivity. A reaction to an ingredient in the diet, which can show up as itchy skin or ears, sometimes with digestive signs. It usually doesn't follow the seasons.
  • Mites and other parasites. Mange (sarcoptic or demodectic) and other mites can cause severe itching, hair loss, and crusting. Sarcoptic mange is contagious and intensely itchy.
  • Skin infections. Bacterial infections (pyoderma) and yeast (Malassezia) often develop on top of another problem once the skin barrier is irritated, and they can make the itching much worse, with redness, odor, or greasy patches.
  • Dry skin and contact irritants. Low humidity, over-bathing, or contact with something irritating can leave skin flaky and itchy.
  • Less commonly, hormonal (endocrine) conditions change the skin and set the stage for infection, and stress or boredom can drive repetitive licking. These are for your vet to sort out.

Red flags — call your vet now

Get veterinary help promptly if you notice any of these:

  • Facial swelling, hives, or trouble breathing — this can be a serious allergic reaction. Seek emergency care immediately.
  • Skin that is raw, bleeding, oozing, or foul-smelling, or a fast-spreading red, wet patch (a "hot spot").
  • Itching so intense it stops your dog from sleeping or eating, or your dog is injuring themselves by scratching, licking, or chewing.
  • Spreading hair loss, bald patches, or thickened, darkened skin.
  • Ear involvement — head-shaking, scratching at the ears, discharge, or odor.
  • Itching alongside low energy, fever, or going off food.

When in doubt, call. It is always reasonable to ask your vet's team whether your dog should be seen.

What to do at home: observe, don't treat

The most useful thing you can do at home is watch closely and write things down — not start a treatment. The cause determines what actually helps, and treating blindly can make your dog worse or hide the very clues your vet needs.

Worth tracking:

  • Where on the body your dog is itchy (paws, ears, belly, rear, all over).
  • How often and how intensely, and whether it's worse at certain times of day or year.
  • How the skin looks — redness, bald spots, scabs, flakes, odor — and whether that's changing.
  • Anything new in the last few weeks: food, treats, shampoo, cleaning products, bedding, walking spots, or travel.
  • Photos and short videos over time, which capture the skin and the scratching far better than memory.

Please do not give human antihistamines or any other medication, and do not apply creams, ointments, shampoos, or home remedies, without your vet's guidance. Some are toxic to dogs, doses differ from people, and self-treating can delay the right care. Decisions about flea and parasite products, bathing routines, diet trials, and medication all belong with your vet — not with this article.

What your vet will ask you

Itchy-skin cases are detective work, and the history you bring is often as valuable as the exam. Come ready to answer:

  • When did it start, and is it constant or coming and going? Does it follow the seasons?
  • Where on the body is it worst, and has that spread?
  • What's changed recently — new food, treats, shampoo, environment, or travel?
  • Your dog's current flea and parasite prevention — which product, and when it was last given.
  • Any other signs — ear trouble, digestive upset, hair loss, odor, or changes in mood or appetite.
  • Anything already given or applied, and whether it seemed to help — so your vet has the full picture.
  • Photos or videos over time, especially of the skin and of your dog scratching.

Frequently asked questions

Why is my dog so itchy but has no fleas?

Itching without visible fleas is common. Environmental allergies (to pollen, dust mites, or mold), food allergies, mites, and skin infections can all cause intense itching with no flea in sight. Fleas can also be present but hard to spot, especially after grooming. Because the cause changes what helps, this is one to work through with your vet rather than guess at.

Should I give my dog an antihistamine for itching?

That's a question for your vet. Many human medications and creams are unsafe for dogs, and giving anything on your own can mask the signs your vet needs to see. Ask your vet before giving any medication or applying anything to your dog's skin.

Can food cause my dog to itch?

Yes. Some dogs react to ingredients in their food with itchy skin or ears, sometimes alongside digestive signs. Confirming a food allergy usually means a carefully run diet trial guided by your vet, not a casual food swap, because changing foods on your own can muddy the picture.

When should I take my itchy dog to the vet?

Call your vet if the itching is constant, getting worse, or stopping your dog from sleeping or eating; if the skin is broken, bleeding, smelly, or has bald patches; or if there is ear trouble or a change in mood or appetite. Facial swelling, hives, or trouble breathing are emergencies — seek care immediately.

This article is general information, not veterinary advice, and it can't diagnose your dog. Every dog is different — when in doubt, call your vet.
